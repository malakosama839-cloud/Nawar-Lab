Mutual & Self Induction

Mutual Induction

Place two coils near each other. When the current in the primary coil changes, its magnetic flux changes — and part of that flux passes through the secondary coil, inducing an EMF in it. This is mutual induction, and it is exactly how a transformer works (next pages).

EMF₂ = − M × ΔI₁ / ΔtM = coefficient of mutual inductance (henry, H); ΔI₁/Δt = rate of change of the primary current (A/s). The minus sign: the induced EMF opposes the change (Lenz)

M depends on the geometry of the two coils: number of turns, area, distance apart, and their orientation. It is measured in henry (H). Putting an iron core through both coils concentrates the flux and greatly increases M.

Self Induction

A coil does not need a neighbour: when its own current changes, its own flux changes, and an EMF is induced in the coil itself. This is self induction. By Lenz's rule the induced EMF always opposes the change of current, so it is called a back-EMF:

EMF = − L × ΔI / ΔtL = self-inductance of the coil (henry, H); ΔI/Δt = rate of change of current (A/s)

The henry (H)

Definition: the self-inductance of a coil is 1 henry if a current changing at the rate of 1 A per second induces in it a back-EMF of 1 volt.

Factors affecting L

  • Number of turns: more turns → larger L (it grows roughly with the square of N).
  • Coil area: larger cross-sectional area → larger L.
  • Core material: an iron core → much larger L than an air core.
Why do we care? A large self-inductance resists sudden changes in current — useful in choke coils and filters, but the reason a switch opening an inductive circuit produces a spark: the back-EMF tries to keep the current flowing.
Worked example: (a) Two coupled coils have M = 0.5 H. The primary current changes at 20 A/s. Find the EMF induced in the secondary.
EMF₂ = M × ΔI₁/Δt = 0.5 × 20 = 10 V
(b) A coil of self-inductance L = 2 H carries a current changing at 3 A/s. Find the back-EMF.
EMF = L × ΔI/Δt = 2 × 3 = 6 V
