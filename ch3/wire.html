Induction in a Moving Wire

A Wire Cutting Field Lines

Imagine a straight wire of length L moving with velocity v through a magnetic field B. As it moves, it sweeps across (cuts) the field lines, so the flux linked with the wire changes — and by Faraday's law an EMF is induced between its ends.

EMF = − B × L × v × sin θB in tesla, L in metres, v in m/s → EMF in volts. θ is the angle between the velocity v and the field B. The minus sign indicates the direction of the EMF (Lenz's rule)

The EMF is maximum when the wire moves perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°, sin θ = 1) and zero when it moves parallel to the field (θ = 0°, sin θ = 0) — because then it cuts no field lines at all. It is also zero if B = 0, L = 0 or v = 0.

Quick link to Faraday: in time Δt the wire sweeps an area L × v × Δt, so ΔΦ = B L v Δt, and EMF = ΔΦ/Δt = B L v. Same physics, different point of view.

Fleming's Right-Hand Rule

This rule gives the direction of the induced current in a moving wire. Stretch the thumb, first finger and second finger of your RIGHT hand so all three are mutually perpendicular:

  • First finger → points along the magnetic field B (First = Field).
  • Thumb → points along the direction of motion v (Thumb = Motion).
  • Second finger → points along the induced current (Second = Current).
Don't mix them up! The right hand is for generators (induced current, "the result"). The left hand (Fleming's left-hand rule, Chapter 2) is for motors (force on a current, "the cause").
Worked example: A wire of length 0.25 m moves at 10 m/s in a field of 0.4 T, with its velocity making 30° with the field. Find the induced EMF.
EMF = B L v sin θ = 0.4 × 0.25 × 10 × sin 30° = 1 × 0.5 = 0.5 V
