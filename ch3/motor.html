The DC Motor

The Generator in Reverse

A generator turns motion into electricity. A motor does the opposite: it feeds a current into a coil inside a magnetic field, and the motor effect (Chapter 2) produces forces on the sides of the coil that make it rotate. Energy conversion: electrical → mechanical.

Each side of the coil of length L carrying current I feels a force F = B I L. The two sides feel forces in opposite directions, forming a couple that turns the coil. The turning effect (torque) is:

τ = B × I × A × N × sin θB in tesla, I in amperes, A = coil area in m², N = turns; θ is the angle between the coil's normal (its axis) and B. Torque in N·m

The torque is maximum when the coil's plane is parallel to B (its normal perpendicular to B, θ = 90°) and zero when the coil's normal aligns with B (θ = 0°).

The Split-Ring Commutator

Without help, the coil would oscillate: after half a turn the forces would pull it back. The split-ring commutator solves this: a ring cut into two halves, each connected to one end of the coil, rubbing against fixed brushes.

How it works: every half turn, each brush slides from one half of the ring to the other, so the current in the coil reverses direction exactly when the coil passes the position where the torque would flip. The result: the torque always pushes the same way, and the coil keeps spinning in one direction.

Factors affecting the speed of the motor

  • Stronger magnetic field B → larger torque → faster.
  • Larger current I (more cells / lower resistance) → faster.
  • More turns N or larger coil area A → faster.
Worked example: A coil of 50 turns, area 0.01 m², carries a current of 3 A in a field of 0.2 T. At the instant when the coil's normal makes 90° with B, find the torque.
τ = B I A N sin θ = 0.2 × 3 × 0.01 × 50 × sin 90° = 0.3 × 1 = 0.3 N·m
