X-Rays

Röntgen's penetrating rays — produced by smashing fast electrons into a metal target.

What Are X-Rays?

X-rays are electromagnetic waves of very short wavelength (≈ 0.01–1 nm — smaller than an atom), produced when fast electrons suddenly lose energy inside matter. Because of their tiny wavelength and high photon energy they:

  • Have very high penetrating power — they pass through flesh, paper, and thin metal.
  • Ionize gases — they knock electrons off gas molecules.
  • Affect photographic plates — the basis of X-ray imaging.
  • Are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields — because they are waves, not charged particles.

Production: The Coolidge Tube

The Coolidge tube (hot-cathode X-ray tube) works like this:

  • A heated filament (cathode) releases electrons by thermionic emission.
  • A very high voltage (10–100 kV) between cathode and anode accelerates the electrons to enormous speeds.
  • They smash into a heavy-melting-point target (tungsten) at the anode.
  • Only about 1% of the electrons' energy leaves as X-rays — the other ~99% becomes heat, so the target is made of tungsten (very high melting point) and is cooled with oil or water.

The Two Kinds of X-Ray Spectra

1. Continuous spectrum (Bremsstrahlung — "braking radiation")

Electrons decelerate abruptly when they plunge into the target, giving up different amounts of energy in different collisions. The result is a continuous range of wavelengths with a smooth hump. An electron that loses all its kinetic energy (e·V) in a single collision emits the highest-frequency (shortest-wavelength) photon — the cutoff:

λmin = h·c / (e·V)the cutoff depends on the tube voltage V only — NOT on the target material

2. Characteristic (line) spectrum

If an incoming electron has enough energy to knock an inner-shell electron out of a target atom, an outer electron falls into the hole and emits a photon of a very exact energy — sharp lines at fixed wavelengths (Kα, Kβ, …). These lines depend on the target material only, not on the voltage — each element has its own set.

Exam tip: λmin → depends on voltage. Characteristic lines → depend on target material. Two different questions examiners ask about the same graph!
Worked example: A Coolidge tube works at V = 40 kV. Find the shortest X-ray wavelength it can emit.
λmin = hc / eV = (6.625×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 4×10⁴)
= 1.9875×10⁻²⁵ / 6.4×10⁻¹⁵ ≈ 3.1×10⁻¹¹ m = 0.031 nm
(Check with the shortcut: λmin(nm) = 1240 / (V in eV) = 1240 / 40 000 = 0.031 nm ✓)
