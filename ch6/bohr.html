Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom

The model that explained why atoms don't collapse — and why each one glows with its own colours.

Rutherford's Model and Its Fatal Problem

Rutherford's model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus with electrons orbiting around it — like a miniature solar system.

The problem: according to classical physics, an orbiting (accelerating) electron must continuously radiate electromagnetic waves. It would steadily lose energy, spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus in a fraction of a second. Every atom should collapse instantly — yet atoms are stable, and they emit light only at certain special frequencies, not continuously. Classical physics could not explain either fact.

Bohr's Two Postulates

In 1913 Niels Bohr rescued the hydrogen atom with two revolutionary postulates:

1. Allowed orbits (stationary states)

The electron moves only in certain allowed circular orbits — called stationary states — and while it stays in one of them it does not radiate any energy. Only orbits whose angular momentum is a whole-number multiple of h/2π are allowed:

m·v·r = n × h / 2πn = 1, 2, 3, … (quantum number) — the angular momentum is quantized

2. Radiation only on jumps

Radiation is emitted (or absorbed) only when the electron jumps from one allowed orbit to another. The photon carries exactly the energy difference between the two levels:

h·f = E₂ − E₁jump down: photon emitted; jump up: photon absorbed

The ground state (n = 1) is the lowest level, with E = −13.6 eV. To pull the electron completely out of the atom (to E = 0 at n = ∞) requires the ionization energy of hydrogen:

Eionization = 0 − (−13.6) = 13.6 eVabout 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom
Worked example: An electron in hydrogen jumps from n = 3 to n = 2. Find the energy of the levels, the photon energy, and its wavelength.
E₃ = −13.6/9 = −1.51 eV ; E₂ = −13.6/4 = −3.40 eV
ΔE = E₃ − E₂ = −1.51 − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV → a photon of 1.89 eV is emitted.
λ = hc/ΔE = 1240 / 1.89 ≈ 656 nm — red visible light (the famous H-alpha line).
