Types of Spectra & the Spectrometer

Three kinds of rainbow — and the instrument that sorts light into its colours.

The Three Types of Spectra

1. Continuous emission spectrum

Produced by a hot, dense source — a glowing solid, liquid, or gas under high pressure (a lamp filament, molten metal, the Sun's dense inner layers). All wavelengths are present in an unbroken band of colours, so there are no dark or bright lines. The spectrum depends on the temperature of the source, not on what it is made of.

2. Line emission spectrum

Produced by an excited gas at low pressure (a glowing discharge tube or flame test). The atoms emit only their own special wavelengths, so we see bright coloured lines on a dark background. Each element has a unique set of lines — a fingerprint — so line spectra are used to identify elements even from far away.

3. Absorption (dark-line) spectrum

Produced when continuous light passes through a cool gas. The gas absorbs exactly the wavelengths it could emit — so dark lines appear inside the continuous band, at those same characteristic positions. This is how we read the composition of stars: the dark lines in the Sun's spectrum (Fraunhofer lines) tell us which elements exist in its outer atmosphere.

Key connection: the dark lines of an absorption spectrum fall at exactly the same wavelengths as the bright lines of that gas's emission spectrum — because both come from the same energy-level jumps, absorbed on the way up or emitted on the way down.

The Spectrometer

The spectrometer is the instrument that produces and analyses pure spectra. It has three main parts:

  • Collimator: a narrow slit + lens that turns the incoming light into a parallel beam.
  • Prism (or diffraction grating): disperses the beam — each wavelength refracts by a different angle (violet bends most, red least), so the colours separate into a pure spectrum.
  • Telescope: collects the separated rays so each colour is viewed sharply as its own line or band.

Because dispersion separates the wavelengths, the spectrometer lets us measure the exact positions of spectral lines — the data behind Bohr's model and the series of hydrogen.

Quick example: Astronomers photograph the Sun's spectrum and find dark lines at exactly 656 nm, 486 nm, 434 nm and 410 nm.
These match the bright lines of hydrogen's Balmer series — so hydrogen exists in the Sun's atmosphere. Its red 656 nm line (H-alpha) is the same one you computed in the previous topic.
