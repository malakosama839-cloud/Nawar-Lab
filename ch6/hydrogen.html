The Hydrogen Spectrum

Five families of lines — each one a set of electron jumps ending on the same level.

From Jumps to Spectral Lines

Every photon hydrogen emits comes from an electron falling from a higher level to a lower one, with h·f = E₂ − E₁ and λ = 1240/ΔE (nm). Group the jumps by their final (lower) level and the lines fall into five families — the spectral series. Each series is named after its discoverer, and lives in one region of the electromagnetic spectrum:

Only the Balmer series (four bright lines: red, cyan, blue-violet, violet) can be seen with the naked eye — the rest need UV or IR detectors.

Levels Converge at Ionization

Since En = −13.6/n², higher levels have less-negative energies and lie closer and closer together: n = 1 is at −13.6 eV, but n = 8 is already at −0.21 eV. The levels crowd together and converge at E = 0, which is the ionization limit — a free electron. A jump from n = ∞ to n = 1 emits the maximum possible energy, 13.6 eV (the Lyman limit at 91.2 nm).

Worked example: Find the wavelength of the first Balmer line (n = 3 → n = 2).
ΔE = 13.6 × (1/2² − 1/3²) = 13.6 × (1/4 − 1/9) = 13.6 × 5/36 = 1.89 eV
λ = 1240 / 1.89 ≈ 656 nm — red light (H-alpha), the strongest line in the visible hydrogen spectrum.
