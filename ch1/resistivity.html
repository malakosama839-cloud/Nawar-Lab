Resistivity, Conductivity, Power & Energy

Resistivity (ρ)

Every material has an intrinsic opposition to current called resistivity (ρ) — it depends on the material itself, not on the shape of the wire. But the resistance of an actual wire depends on both the material and its dimensions:

R = ρ × L / AL = wire length (m), A = cross-section area (m²), ρ = resistivity (Ω·m)
  • Longer wire → more resistance (current struggles over a longer distance).
  • Thicker wire → less resistance (more room for electrons to pass).

Conductivity (σ)

Conductivity is simply the opposite idea: how easily a material lets current flow.

σ = 1 / ρGood conductor: high σ, low ρ (e.g. copper). Good insulator: low σ, high ρ (e.g. rubber)
MaterialResistivity ρ (Ω·m)Type
Silver1.6 × 10⁻⁸Best conductor
Copper1.7 × 10⁻⁸Conductor (wiring!)
Iron1.0 × 10⁻⁷Conductor
Nichrome1.1 × 10⁻⁶Alloy — heating elements
Rubber~10¹³Insulator
Worked example: A copper wire has length 20 m and cross-section area 1 × 10⁻⁶ m². Find its resistance (ρ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m).
R = ρL/A = (1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 20) / (1 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.34 Ω

Electric Power & Energy

Power (P) is the rate at which a device converts electrical energy into heat, light or motion. Measured in watts (W).

P = V × I = I² × R = V² / RAll three are equivalent — pick whichever matches what you know

Energy (E) is the total amount converted over a period of time. Measured in joules (J) — or in kilowatt-hours (kWh) on your electricity bill.

E = P × tt in seconds gives E in joules; t in hours with P in kW gives E in kWh
Worked example: A heater draws 5 A from a 220 V supply for 2 hours. Find its power and the energy consumed.
P = V × I = 220 × 5 = 1100 W  •  E = P × t = 1100 × 2 × 3600 = 7.92 × 10⁶ J (= 2.2 kWh)
