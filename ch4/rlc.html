RL, RC and RLC Series Circuits

Same current everywhere — but the voltages no longer add like numbers. They add like vectors.

Why Voltages Add as Vectors

In a series circuit the same current I flows through every component. But the voltages across them are not in step: VR is in phase with I, VL leads I by 90°, VC lags I by 90°. So the total voltage is found by vector addition:

V² = VR² + (VL − VC)²V_L and V_C point in opposite directions, so they partly cancel each other

Dividing every term by I gives the total opposition to AC — the impedance Z:

Z = √( R² + (XL − XC)² )impedance in ohms — and the AC version of Ohm's law is V = I × Z
tan φ = (XL − XC) / Rφ = phase angle between the total voltage V and the current I
  • φ positive → the circuit is inductive: XL > XC, the voltage leads the current (RL behaviour).
  • φ negative → the circuit is capacitive: XC > XL, the current leads the voltage (RC behaviour).
  • φ = 0 → XL = XC: the circuit behaves as pure resistance — this is resonance.

The Three Series Circuits at a Glance

CircuitImpedance Ztan φNature
RL (coil + R)√(R² + XL²)XL / R  (positive)Inductive — V leads I
RC (capacitor + R)√(R² + XC²)−XC / R  (negative)Capacitive — I leads V
RLC (all three)√(R² + (XL − XC)²)(XL − XC) / RDepends on which reactance wins
Worked example: A coil of reactance XL = 40 Ω and a resistor R = 30 Ω are in series across a 220 V AC supply (XC = 0).
Z = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 Ω
I = V / Z = 220 / 50 = 4.4 A
tan φ = 40 / 30 = 1.333 → φ ≈ 53° — positive, so the circuit is inductive: the voltage leads the current by 53°.
