Magnetic Fields from Currents

Oersted's discovery: every electric current is surrounded by a magnetic field.

The Right-Hand Grip Rule

When a current flows, a magnetic field appears around it. To find the field's direction, imagine gripping the wire with your right hand so your thumb points along the current: your curled fingers then point in the direction of the magnetic field lines circling the wire.

Exam trap: it is the right hand for finding the field around a current, but Fleming's rule for the force on a wire uses the left hand. Don't mix them up!

Field of a Straight Wire

A straight wire carrying current I produces circular field lines centred on the wire. At a distance r from the wire (Ampère's circuital law):

B = μ₀ × I / (2π r)μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A (permeability of air/vacuum), I in amperes, r in metres

Notice that B is proportional to I and inversely proportional to r — double the distance and the field halves.

Neutral points

The Earth itself has a magnetic field (BEarth ≈ 40 μT horizontally). When a wire's field at some point is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the Earth's field, the two cancel and the net field is zero. Such a point is called a neutral point, and there:

Bwire = BEarth → μ₀ I / (2π r) = BEarthused to find the distance r of the neutral point from the wire

Field at the Centre of a Circular Coil

Bending the wire into a coil of N turns of radius r, every turn adds its field at the centre (all pointing the same way):

B = μ₀ × N × I / (2 r)N = number of turns, r = coil radius in metres — more turns or a smaller coil gives a stronger field

Field Inside a Solenoid

A solenoid is a long wire wound into many close circular turns — an electromagnet. Inside a long solenoid the field is uniform (parallel, equally spaced lines), like the field inside a bar magnet; outside it resembles a bar magnet's field with N and S poles at the ends.

B = μ₀ × n × In = number of turns per metre = N / L (L = solenoid length in m). The field inside is uniform and does not depend on position
Exam trap: for a solenoid use n = N/L (turns per metre), not N itself. Always convert the length to metres first!
Worked example: A solenoid has 500 turns over a length of 0.25 m and carries a current of 2 A. Find B inside.
n = N / L = 500 / 0.25 = 2000 turns/m
B = μ₀ n I = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 2000 × 2 = 8π×10⁻⁴ ≈ 5.03 × 10⁻³ T = 5.03 mT
Worked example (neutral point): A long straight wire carries 50 A. Where is the neutral point if the Earth's horizontal field is 4 × 10⁻⁵ T?
Set μ₀I / (2πr) = BEarth: r = μ₀I / (2π BEarth) = (2×10⁻⁷ × 50) / (4×10⁻⁵) = 10⁻⁵ / (4×10⁻⁵) = 0.25 m
