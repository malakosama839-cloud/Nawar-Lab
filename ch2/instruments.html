Measuring Instruments

The Moving-Coil Galvanometer

The galvanometer detects and measures small currents (μA – mA). Its main parts:

  • A rectangular coil of many fine turns suspended between the poles of a strong permanent magnet.
  • A radial magnetic field — the pole pieces are curved so the field always lies along the plane of the coil (the plane stays parallel to B as it turns). Then θ = 90° in τ = B·I·A·N·sinθ at every position, so the deflecting torque τ = B·I·A·N is constant and does not depend on the angle.
  • Two hairsprings that supply the current and provide a restoring torque proportional to the deflection angle (τrestoring = c·φ).

At equilibrium, c·φ = B·I·A·N, so the deflection angle φ is directly proportional to the current — the scale is linear (equal divisions).

φ = (B × A × N / c) × Ic = torsion constant of the spring. The bracket is the current sensitivity

Sensitivity (larger deflection for the same current) increases with B, N and coil area A, and with a weaker spring (smaller c). A galvanometer has a relatively high resistance compared to an ammeter.

DC Ammeter — the Shunt Resistor

A galvanometer alone cannot measure large currents (its coil would burn). We connect a very small resistance S (the shunt) in parallel with it, so most of the current bypasses the coil:

S = Ig × G / (I − Ig)G = galvanometer resistance, I_g = full-scale (maximum) galvanometer current, I = max current to be measured

The ideal ammeter has zero resistance so it does not disturb the circuit — that is why S must be very small.

DC Voltmeter — the Multiplier Resistor

A galvanometer can read small voltages only (V = Ig·G). To measure larger voltages, connect a large resistance Rm (the multiplier) in series, which takes most of the voltage drop:

Rm = V / Ig − GV = maximum voltage to be measured. At full scale the current through G is still exactly I_g

The ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance (draws no current) — that is why Rm must be very large.

Exam trap: ammeter → shunt in parallel (small S); voltmeter → multiplier in series (large Rm). Mixing them up is the most common exam mistake in this chapter!

The Ohmmeter

  • An ohmmeter is a galvanometer with its own battery and a fixed series resistor inside the box.
  • Zero adjustment: before measuring, the two terminals are shorted (R = 0) and the zero-adjuster changes the resistance so the current is full-scale — i.e. full deflection means 0 Ω.
  • An unknown resistance R reduces the current, so the scale reads backwards: large deflection = small R, and zero deflection (open circuit) = infinite R.
  • The meter is calibrated so that mid-scale (half deflection) equals the meter's own internal resistance — that is how the internal resistance is found.
Worked example: A galvanometer has G = 50 Ω and full-scale current Ig = 1 mA. Convert it to (a) an ammeter reading up to 1 A, (b) a voltmeter reading up to 10 V.
(a) Shunt: S = Ig·G / (I − Ig) = (0.001 × 50) / (1 − 0.001) = 0.05 / 0.999 ≈ 0.05 Ω
(b) Multiplier: Rm = V/Ig − G = 10 / 0.001 − 50 = 10000 − 50 = 9950 Ω
