Torque on a Coil & Magnetic Dipole Moment

A Couple of Forces

Put a rectangular coil carrying current I inside a field B. The two long sides carry currents in opposite directions, so Fleming's left-hand rule gives them opposite forces of equal size F = B·I·L (the sides ⊥ to B). These two equal, opposite, non-aligned forces form a couple that spins the coil — a torque (τ). This is the working principle of the electric motor and the moving-coil galvanometer.

Torque on a Rectangular Coil

For a coil of N turns of area A = length × width, carrying current I in a field B, where θ is the angle between the NORMAL (the axis) of the coil and B:

τ = B × I × A × N × sin θτ in newton·metre (N·m). θ = angle between the coil's axis (normal) and B
  • Maximum torque τ = B·I·A·N when θ = 90°, i.e. the coil's plane is parallel to the field (the normal is ⊥ B). This is where the motor effect on the two sides is largest.
  • Zero torque when θ = 0°, i.e. the coil's plane is perpendicular to the field (the normal is parallel to B) — the forces then merely stretch the coil instead of rotating it.
Exam trap: in the torque formula θ is measured from the coil's normal (axis), so maximum torque happens when the plane of the coil is parallel to B — not perpendicular!
Worked example: A rectangular coil of 100 turns measures 0.2 m × 0.1 m and carries 0.5 A in a field of 0.3 T. Find (a) the maximum torque, (b) the torque when the coil's plane makes 60° with B.
A = 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02 m²; m (dipole moment) = N·I·A = 100 × 0.5 × 0.02 = 1 A·m²
(a) Plane parallel to B → θ = 90°: τ = 0.3 × 0.5 × 0.02 × 100 × sin90° = 0.3 N·m
(b) Plane at 60° to B → normal at θ = 30° to B: τ = 0.3 × 0.5 × 0.02 × 100 × sin30° = 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15 N·m

Magnetic Dipole Moment

The product N·I·A depends only on the coil itself, not on the field. It measures how strong a "magnet" the coil behaves like — the magnetic dipole moment:

m = N × I × Aunit: ampere·metre² (A·m²). Then τ = m × B × sin θ — a shorter form of the torque law

A current loop in a field behaves exactly like a small bar magnet: its dipole moment points along the normal to the coil (right-hand grip rule), and the field tries to line it up with B. That is why the torque vanishes when the normal is parallel to B (θ = 0).
