The p-n Junction (Diode)

Joining p to n: the Depletion Region

Take a p-type crystal (mostly holes) and an n-type crystal (mostly electrons) and join them in one piece. Right at the junction:

  • Electrons diffuse from the n-side into the p-side and fill holes near the border.
  • The region near the junction is left with no free carriers — only fixed charged ions. This is the depletion region ("depleted" of carriers).
  • The fixed charges create a small internal voltage called the barrier potential, which opposes any further diffusion.
Vbarrier ≈ 0.7 V for silicon (≈ 0.3 V for germanium)the "door" a charge must push through to cross the junction

Forward Bias vs Reverse Bias

Forward bias → conducts

Connect the battery + to p, − to n. The external voltage pushes carriers toward the junction and the barrier shrinks. Once the supply passes about 0.7 V (silicon), current flows easily.

Reverse bias → blocks

Connect the battery − to p, + to n. Carriers are pulled away from the junction and the barrier and depletion region grow wider. Only a tiny leakage current flows.

So the diode passes current in one direction only — it behaves as a one-way valve for electricity. That single property makes it enormously useful.

Remember: the diode starts to conduct only when the forward voltage exceeds the barrier potential (~0.7 V for Si). Below that, almost nothing flows.

Rectification: AC into DC

An AC source pushes current backwards and forwards alternately. Put one diode in series with the load: it passes the positive halves of the wave and blocks the negative halves. The load receives pulses of current that all flow the same way — this is half-wave rectification.

  • One diode → half-wave rectification (half of each cycle is lost).
  • A bridge of four diodes → full-wave rectification: the two halves of the cycle are steered so both reach the load in the same direction.
Worked example: A silicon diode is connected in forward bias in series with a 6 V battery and a 100 Ω resistor. Find the current.
The diode "uses up" its barrier potential: voltage across the resistor = 6 − 0.7 = 5.3 V.
I = V / R = 5.3 / 100 = 0.053 A = 53 mA
(If the same diode were reverse biased, the current would be only a tiny leakage ≈ 0.)
