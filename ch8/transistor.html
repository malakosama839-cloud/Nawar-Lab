The Transistor

Structure: Two Junctions, Three Terminals

A transistor is made of three semiconductor layers joined back to back, so it contains two p-n junctions. The layers can be arranged as npn or pnp. Each layer has a name and a terminal:

  • Emitter (E): heavily doped — its job is to emit (send) the charge carriers through the device.
  • Base (B): very thin and lightly doped — the middle layer that controls how many carriers get through.
  • Collector (C): moderately doped and larger — it collects the carriers arriving from the emitter.

The two circuits that connect a transistor are the common base and the common emitter connections (the "common" terminal is the one shared by input and output).

Remember the doping: Emitter = heavily doped, Base = thin & lightly doped, Collector = largest area. E-B and C-B are the two junctions.

The Action: a Small Current Controls a Big One

The magic of the transistor: a small base current allows a much larger collector current to flow from emitter to collector. The ratio between them is called the current gain, β (beta):

β = Ic / Ib   →   Ic = β × IbIb = base current, Ic = collector current; β is typically 20 to 200
Ie = Ib + IcKirchhoff's current law: the emitter carries whatever enters through base and collector

As an amplifier

A tiny changing signal fed to the base produces a large, identical change of collector current — so a small input appears greatly enlarged at the output. A weak microphone signal, for example, can drive a loudspeaker.

As a switch

  • No base current → collector current ≈ 0 → the transistor is OFF.
  • Saturating the base (a large enough base current) → the transistor passes its full collector current → ON.

Billions of such switches, flipping on and off billions of times per second, are the heart of every computer processor.

Worked example: A transistor has β = 100 and a base current Ib = 20 μA. Find the collector current and the emitter current.
Ic = β × Ib = 100 × 20 μA = 2000 μA = 2 mA
Ie = Ib + Ic = 0.02 mA + 2 mA = 2.02 mA
