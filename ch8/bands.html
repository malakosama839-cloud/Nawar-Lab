Energy Bands & Semiconductors

Energy Bands in Solids

In a single isolated atom, electrons sit at exact energy levels. But in a solid, billions of atoms are packed close together, and each atom influences its neighbours. The separate levels merge into wide ranges of allowed energy called energy bands.

  • Valence band: the band filled with the electrons that are bound to the atoms. Electrons here cannot move through the crystal.
  • Conduction band: the higher band. An electron that reaches it is free to wander through the material — that is what makes electric current.
  • Forbidden gap (Eg): the energy range between the two bands where no electron may exist. To become free, an electron must gain at least Eg of energy (from heat or light).
Key idea: whether a material conducts depends on one question — how wide is the gap between the valence band and the conduction band?

Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

Conductors (copper, iron)

The conduction band overlaps the valence band — there is no gap at all. Plenty of electrons are always free, so current flows easily.

Insulators (glass, diamond)

A huge forbidden gap of about 5 eV or more. Almost no electron can ever gather enough energy to cross it, so the material does not conduct.

Semiconductors (silicon, germanium)

A small gap of about 1 eV. At room temperature some electrons manage to jump it — so a semiconductor conducts better than an insulator but worse than a metal.

Intrinsic Semiconductors (Pure)

An intrinsic semiconductor is a perfectly pure crystal of silicon or germanium. When an electron gains enough thermal energy to jump into the conduction band, it leaves an empty place in the valence band called a hole. A hole behaves exactly like a positive charge carrier — nearby electrons fall into it, so the hole appears to move the opposite way.

ne = nhin a pure semiconductor: number of free electrons = number of holes (they are created in pairs)
  • Every electron promoted to the conduction band leaves exactly one hole behind.
  • Heating the crystal creates more electron–hole pairs → conductivity rises with temperature (the opposite of metals, whose resistance rises).
  • At room temperature a pure semiconductor still conducts poorly — only a few pairs exist.

Extrinsic Semiconductors — Doping

Doping means adding a tiny amount (about one atom in a million) of a chosen impurity to a pure semiconductor. This gives the crystal a controlled number of extra carriers — it becomes extrinsic.

n-type (pentavalent donor)

Add a pentavalent atom (5 valence electrons) such as arsenic, phosphorus or antimony. Four of its electrons bond with neighbouring silicon atoms — the fifth is left over and becomes a free electron.

Majority carriers = electronsholes are the minority carriers

p-type (trivalent acceptor)

Add a trivalent atom (3 valence electrons) such as indium, boron or aluminium. One bond is left incomplete — each impurity atom creates a hole.

Majority carriers = holeselectrons are the minority carriers
Tip: doped crystals are still electrically neutral — the impurity atoms are neutral too. "n-type" and "p-type" refer only to which carrier is in the majority, not to any net charge.
Worked example: Compare the jump an electron must make in germanium with the jump in diamond.
Germanium: Eg ≈ 0.7 eV. Diamond: Eg ≈ 5.5 eV.
Ratio = 5.5 / 0.7 ≈ 8 times larger — that is why at room temperature germanium conducts (some electrons cross 0.7 eV) while diamond does not (no electron can cross 5.5 eV).
