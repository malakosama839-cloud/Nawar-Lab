Elements of the Laser

Every laser — gas, crystal or semiconductor — is built from the same five ingredients.

The Five Elements

① The active medium

The material with the right energy levels that actually amplifies the light: a helium–neon gas mixture, a ruby crystal, a semiconductor diode… The medium often works with a three-level (or four-level) scheme: atoms are lifted to a short-lived upper level, then fall quickly into a long-lived metastable state and pile up there, waiting to be stimulated.

② The pumping source

Something must lift the atoms up continuously. This is called pumping, and it can be:

  • Optical pumping — a powerful flash lamp shining intense light on the medium (e.g. ruby laser).
  • Electrical pumping — an electric discharge (a current) through the gas, e.g. in the He-Ne laser.

③ The metastable state

Ordinary excited states last only ~10⁻⁸ s — far too short to build up a population. A metastable state is an excited state with a much longer lifetime, so pumped atoms accumulate in it instead of spontaneously falling straight back down. Without it, population inversion would be impossible.

④ Population inversion

Normally most atoms sit in the low level, so a passing photon is more likely to be absorbed than to stimulate emission. Pumping hard enough puts more atoms in the excited (metastable) level than in the lower level — an inverted population. Only then does stimulated emission win over absorption and the medium amplifies light.

⑤ The resonant cavity (two parallel mirrors)

The active medium is closed between two exactly parallel mirrors: one reflects 100%, the other is partially transparent (~98%). Photons bounce back and forth thousands of times, stimulating more and more emissions on every pass — and the small fraction that leaks through the half-silvered mirror escapes as the laser beam.

Worked example: Start with 1 photon bouncing in the cavity. On each pass, every photon stimulates one identical twin, so the number of photons doubles per pass.
After 1 pass: 2 → after 2 passes: 4 → after 3 passes: 8 … after n passes: 2n photons.
After 10 passes: 2¹⁰ = 1024 photons. After 20 passes: 2²⁰ ≈ 1.05 million — this explosive growth is why a laser beam is so intense.

Exam tip: Order of events: pump → population inversion (needs the metastable state) → stimulated emission → mirrors recycle photons → beam escapes through the partially transparent mirror. If an exam question asks "why is the metastable state essential?", the answer is: it gives excited atoms a long lifetime, allowing the population inversion without which absorption beats amplification.
