Spontaneous vs Stimulated Emission

Three things an atom can do with a photon — and the third one is the secret behind every laser.

The Three Processes

1. Absorption

A photon whose energy exactly matches the gap — hf = E₂ − E₁ — is absorbed by the atom, and the atom jumps from the lower level E₁ to the upper level E₂. The photon disappears.

2. Spontaneous emission

An excited atom is unstable: after about 10⁻⁸ s it falls back to the lower level on its own and emits a photon of energy hf = E₂ − E₁. But the direction and phase are completely random — different atoms emit photons that have nothing to do with each other. This is what ordinary light sources (lamps, the sun, neon signs) produce.

3. Stimulated emission — the laser's engine

If a passing photon with hf = E₂ − E₁ flies past an already excited atom, it triggers the atom to fall and emit a second photon — and the twin is identical to the triggering photon: same frequency, same direction, same phase. One photon in, two identical photons out; two become four, four become eight… that is light amplification — the "LA" in laser.

Worked example: An atom has two levels separated by ΔE = 1.96 eV (as in neon). What wavelength of photon can be absorbed — or stimulate the emission of another?
λ = hc/ΔE = 1240 / 1.96 ≈ 633 nm — red light. Only photons of this exact wavelength (frequency f = ΔE/h) can interact with this transition.

Amplification

Only stimulated emission copies a photon: two identical photons leave instead of one. That copying — over and over, inside a mirrored cavity — is what turns dim light into a powerful, perfectly ordered laser beam.

Why Only Stimulated Light Can Make a Laser

Exam tip: The triggering photon is not consumed in stimulated emission — it leaves together with its newborn identical twin. Compare with absorption, where the photon vanishes completely.
