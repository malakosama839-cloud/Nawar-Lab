Black Body Radiation & Quanta

Where quantum physics began: a hot glow that classical physics could not explain — until Planck took a daring idea seriously.

What Is a Black Body?

A black body is an ideal object that absorbs all radiation falling on it — it reflects nothing. Because it is in thermal equilibrium, it is also a perfect emitter: it re-emits radiation with a spectrum that depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of. A good real-life approximation is a small hole into a hot cavity, or the glowing filament of a lamp — and stars behave very much like black bodies.

As you heat a body: it first glows dull red, then bright orange-yellow, and if hot enough it turns white-blue. The colour change is a clue about the radiation's spectrum.

The Ultraviolet Catastrophe

Classical physics predicted that a hot body should radiate an unlimited amount of energy at high frequencies — the curve keeps rising into the ultraviolet forever. Experiment disagrees: the curve rises, peaks, then falls. This total failure of the classical prediction is remembered as the "ultraviolet catastrophe": classical theory wrongly predicted infinite radiation at short wavelengths.

Planck's Hypothesis — Energy Comes in Quanta

In 1900 Max Planck fixed the problem with a revolutionary assumption: matter does not exchange energy continuously — it exchanges it in discrete packets called quanta. A body can emit or absorb one quantum, two quanta, three quanta… but never a fraction of a quantum. The energy of one quantum is proportional to the frequency of the radiation:

E = h × fh = 6.625 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant), f = frequency of the radiation (Hz)

Higher frequency ⇒ bigger quanta. That is why hot bodies do not pour out unlimited ultraviolet energy: a single ultraviolet quantum costs a lot of energy, and the atoms simply cannot afford many of them.

Worked example: Find the energy (in joules and in eV) of one quantum of radiation with frequency f = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
E = h·f = 6.625×10⁻³⁴ × 6×10¹⁴ = 3.975 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
In electron-volts: E = 3.975×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.5 eV

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed c = 3×10⁸ m/s, but they differ in frequency (and therefore wavelength, since c = λ·f). Ordered by increasing frequency:

Radio → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays. Since E = h·f, gamma photons carry far more energy than radio photons.

Wien's Displacement Law

Look at the black-body curve: it has a peak at one particular wavelength λmax. As the temperature rises, the whole curve grows and its peak shifts toward shorter wavelengths (bluer light). Wien's law links them:

λmax × T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m·Kλ_max in metres, T in kelvin — hotter ⇒ peak shifts bluer
  • A cool star (3000 K) peaks in the infrared/red → looks reddish.
  • The Sun (≈ 5800 K) peaks in the visible → looks yellow-white.
  • A very hot star (10 000 K+) peaks in the ultraviolet → looks blue-white.
Worked example: The Sun's surface temperature is about 5800 K. Find the wavelength of its peak radiation.
λmax = 2.9×10⁻³ / T = 2.9×10⁻³ / 5800 = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm — right in the visible (green-blue) region, which is why the Sun looks yellow-white and our eyes evolved to see this range.
