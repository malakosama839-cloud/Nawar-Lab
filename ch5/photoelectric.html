The Photoelectric Effect

How light knocks electrons out of metal — and why only Einstein's photons could explain the experiment.

Getting Electrons Out of Metal: Thermionic Emission

Free electrons inside a metal are held back by the surface — they need energy to escape. One way to give it to them is heat: heating a metal (e.g. a cathode filament) gives its free electrons enough energy to leave the surface. This is called thermionic emission ("thermal electron emission").

The cathode ray tube (CRT)

The CRT uses thermionic emission to produce a beam of electrons:

  • Heated cathode: the filament boils off electrons (thermionic emission).
  • Anode: kept at a high positive voltage, it accelerates the electrons forward through a small hole — a fast cathode ray (electron beam).
  • Deflecting plates: electric fields between parallel plates steer the beam up/down and left/right onto the screen (old TVs and oscilloscopes).

The Photoelectric Effect & the Photoelectric Cell

Another way to free electrons is with light. When light of high enough frequency shines on a metal surface, electrons are ejected immediately — this is the photoelectric effect, and the ejected electrons are called photoelectrons.

The photoelectric cell puts this to work: light hits a photosensitive cathode, the emitted electrons are collected by an anode, and a small current flows whenever light falls on the cell. Brighter light ⇒ more current. Uses: automatic doors, burglar alarms, camera exposure meters.

The Puzzling Experimental Facts

Wave theory of light could not explain what experiments showed:

  • No emission below the threshold frequency — no matter how intense the light is, if f < f₀ not a single electron escapes. (Waves say: brighter light should eventually do the job!)
  • Maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency, not intensity — brighter light ejects more electrons, but not faster ones. Only raising the frequency makes them faster.
  • Emission is instantaneous — electrons leave the instant light hits, with no time delay, even for very dim light.

Einstein's Photon Explanation

In 1905 Einstein used Planck's quanta: light travels as particles — photons — each carrying energy E = h·f. One photon is absorbed by one electron in a single event. This explains everything:

  • A photon either has enough energy to free an electron or it does not — intensity (number of photons) is irrelevant below the threshold. Hence the threshold frequency f₀.
  • Each photon delivers its whole energy at once ⇒ instantaneous emission.
  • More photons per second ⇒ more electrons, but each electron's energy depends only on the photon's frequency.

The work function (Ew or φ) is the minimum energy needed to free one electron from the metal's surface. At the threshold, the photon energy just equals it:

h × f₀ = Ewthreshold frequency f₀ = E_w / h — below f₀, no emission at any intensity

If the photon has more energy than the work function, the leftover becomes kinetic energy of the electron — Einstein's photoelectric equation:

K.E = E − Ew = h·f − h·f₀this is the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons (Nobel Prize 1921)

The fastest electrons can be stopped by a reverse voltage Vs (the stopping potential), which measures their kinetic energy:

e × Vs = K.Ee = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C — so a stopping potential of 1.1 V means K.E = 1.1 eV
Worked example: Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal with work function Ew = 2.0 eV.
Photon energy: E = hc/λ = 1240 / 400 = 3.1 eV > Ew ⇒ emission happens.
Maximum kinetic energy: K.E = E − Ew = 3.1 − 2.0 = 1.1 eV, so the stopping potential Vs = 1.1 V.
Threshold: λ₀ = 1240/2.0 = 620 nm, so f₀ = c/λ₀ = 3×10⁸ / 620×10⁻⁹ ≈ 4.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Any light with λ > 620 nm ejects nothing, however bright.
