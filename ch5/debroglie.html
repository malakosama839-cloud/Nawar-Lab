De Broglie's Relation & the Electron Microscope

If waves can behave like particles, then particles must behave like waves. Louis de Broglie's symmetrical idea.

Matter Waves

Planck and Einstein showed that waves (light) act like particles (photons). In 1924 the young Louis de Broglie asked the natural reverse question: if waves can act like particles, why can't particles act like waves? He proposed that every moving particle is accompanied by a wave whose wavelength is:

λ = h / p = h / (m × v)p = momentum of the particle. Bigger momentum ⇒ shorter wavelength

For everyday objects (a football, a car) the wavelength is unimaginably small — far too small to ever notice. But for a tiny electron moving fast, the wavelength becomes comparable to atomic spacings and can be measured.

An electron accelerated by a voltage V

An electron accelerated from rest through a potential difference V gains kinetic energy e·V = ½m·v². Solving for v and substituting into de Broglie's relation gives a very useful exam formula:

λ = h / √(2 m e V)m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C — note λ ∝ 1/√V
Worked example: An electron is accelerated through 100 V. Find its de Broglie wavelength.
Denominator: √(2meV) = √(2 × 9.1×10⁻³¹ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 100) = √(2.912×10⁻⁴⁷) ≈ 5.4×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s
λ = h / √(2meV) = 6.625×10⁻³⁴ / 5.4×10⁻²⁴ ≈ 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 1.23 Å — about the size of an atom!

Experimental Proof — Electron Diffraction

If electrons really are waves, they should diffract like light does. In experiments of the Davisson–Germer type, a beam of electrons fired at a crystal produced a diffraction pattern (rings of bright and dark spots on a screen), exactly as X-rays do — and the measured wavelength matched λ = h/mv. A wave passing through a particle-free crystal could never do that: electrons are waves too.

The Electron Microscope

A microscope can never resolve details smaller than roughly one wavelength of the radiation it uses. An optical microscope works with visible light (λ ≈ 400–700 nm), so it stops at around 200 nm — smaller objects are simply invisible to it.

De Broglie's idea gives a way out: accelerate electrons and their wavelength shrinks to thousandths of a nanometre. Because the electron's wavelength is thousands of times shorter than visible light's, the electron microscope has far better resolving power (resolving power improves as wavelength gets shorter) and can image viruses, molecules, and even individual atoms.

Exam tip: to improve resolution, use a shorter wavelength. For electrons that means a higher accelerating voltage, since λ = h/√(2meV): ten times the voltage makes the electron √10 ≈ 3.2 times faster and its wavelength √10 times shorter.
